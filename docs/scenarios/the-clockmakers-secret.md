# The Clockmaker's Secret — Scenario Documentation

## Story Synopsis

You're a small creature — goblin-sized, barely reaching a human's knee — who has slipped through a crack in the baseboard of Elias Thorne's clockmaker's workshop. The shop is closed for the night. Candles burn low on every surface. Dozens of clocks tick in near-unison, filling the room with a layered, hypnotic rhythm.

Elias Thorne, master clockmaker, is dead. He slumped forward at his workbench sometime in the last few hours — a half-finished letter beneath his hand, spectacles still on his nose. His final creation dominates the back wall: an astronomical clock six feet tall, all brass and crystal and hand-engraved celestial charts. It still ticks. The other clocks in the shop are winding down — but this one runs with a life of its own.

A letter on the workbench tells the story. Elias spent his final years building this clock for his estranged daughter, Clara. Hidden inside is a mechanical nightingale — a love letter in gears and springs, carrying a scroll with the location of an inheritance she doesn't know exists. But the clock is locked, and Elias designed it so that only someone who truly understood his work could open it. The puzzles aren't traps — they're proof of devotion.

You have until midnight. That's when Marcus, Elias's apprentice, returns. Marcus doesn't know about the nightingale, and if he finds you rummaging through his master's shop, things will go badly. The tower clock in the square outside chimes the quarter-hours — each chime is a reminder that time is running out.

Your size is your advantage. You can crawl inside the clock's mechanism, reach into gaps between gears, peer into spaces no human hand could explore. The shop was built for someone five times your height — workbenches are cliffs, drawers are caves, and the astronomical clock is a cathedral of brass.

**Difficulty**: Medium | **Estimated Time**: 15 minutes | **Patterns Used**: 10

> **Note**: This scenario has no antagonist AI. Tension comes from a time-based narrative structure — quarter-hour chimes mark the passage of time, and the story escalates in urgency, but there is no fail-state timer. The player can take as long as they need. The chimes are atmosphere, not punishment.

## Game Flow

### Act 1: Exploration (Views 1-2, 5, 7)

You enter through a crack in the baseboard and find yourself in the workshop. The immediate goal is to understand where you are and what happened.

1. **Explore workshop_main** — discover the scope of the shop: workbench, display cabinet, astronomical clock, back room curtain
2. **Look at Elias** (object in workshop_main) — learn he has passed away, notice the letter beneath his hand
3. **Navigate to workbench_closeup** — examine the letter to learn the story: Elias was building something for his daughter Clara, hidden in the astronomical clock
4. **Take the locket** from the workbench — it's Clara's locket, the portrait matches the daguerreotype on the wall
5. **Navigate to display_cabinet** — see the music box, mechanical bird, pocket watch. Notice the empty shelf with a dust outline
6. **Navigate to back_room** — discover the star chart and locked tool chest

### Act 2: Understanding (Views 3-4, 6, 8-9)

Now you know the goal: open the clock's compartment. You need to decipher how.

7. **Navigate to astronomical_clock** — examine the clock up close, discover the two keyholes (round and heart-shaped), the zodiac ring, and the jeweled stars on the face
8. **Navigate to clock_face_closeup** — examine the star map on the dial, notice the jeweled stars and the zodiac ring. Can't adjust anything yet — need a special tool
9. **Navigate to star_chart_closeup** — read Elias's star chart, discover seven numbered/circled stars and the cryptic note: "The melody remembers what the mind forgets"
10. **Take the music box** from the display cabinet

### Key Dynamics

- **No Noise System**: This scenario uses no noise/antagonist mechanic. Tension is narrative (the approaching midnight, the urgency of Elias's unfinished business) rather than mechanical.
- **Knowledge Gating**: The music box melody, star chart positions, and letter cipher all gate progress through understanding rather than inventory. You can have every item and still be stuck if you haven't read the clues.
- **Zoom-In Exploration**: Four of the ten views are close-ups of objects (workbench, clock face, music box, star chart, tool chest). This mirrors the feel of point-and-click classics where zooming into a desk or device was a key part of puzzle solving.
- **Emotional Storytelling**: Every item tells a piece of Elias and Clara's story. The locket, the letter, the dedication in the music box, the daguerreotype — the puzzle is also a narrative about a father's love.
- **The Moral Fork**: The ending choice isn't good-vs-evil. It's selfishness vs. empathy vs. compromise. None of the three endings is "wrong" — they just reveal different values.

## Design Notes

### Why This Scenario Works as a Showcase

1. **Visual Impact**: Every view is beautiful — brass, candlelight, celestial mechanisms. There's no gore, no horror, no institutional decay. New users see warmth, wonder, and craftsmanship.

2. **Puzzle Variety**: Seven distinct puzzle types (gateway, knowledge gate, observation, sequence, combination lock, mechanical alignment, moral choice) in just 10 views. This demonstrates the engine's range without overwhelming the player.

3. **Zoom-In Design**: Four of ten views are close-ups. This showcases the engine's ability to handle nested navigation — workshop → workbench → letter, or workshop → clock → clock face → zodiac ring. It's the "explore a desk" feeling that sells point-and-click adventures.

4. **Emotional Narrative**: The story has heart. Elias's love for Clara, the undelivered message, the music box dedication — these details transform puzzles from mechanical exercises into meaningful discoveries. Players remember stories, not systems.

5. **No Antagonist Required**: The scenario works perfectly without killer AI. This proves the engine isn't just a horror game framework — it's a general-purpose adventure game platform.

6. **Approachable Difficulty**: Medium difficulty, ~15 minutes, no fail states. A new user can finish this scenario on their first try with careful observation. The puzzles reward attention, not trial-and-error.

7. **"Just JSON" Factor**: Ten gorgeous views, seven interconnected puzzles, three distinct endings, a complete emotional story — all from a single JSON file and ten background images. That's the pitch.

### Schema Considerations

- **No killer object**: The scenario has no antagonist. The `killer` key in the JSON should be omitted entirely (not set to null — absent).
- **No phases**: Without a killer, there's no phase escalation. The `phases` key can be omitted or contain a single "exploration" phase.
- **Puzzle widgets**: Two are needed — the combination lock (three-dial number entry) and the zodiac ring alignment (rotational alignment puzzle). Both are `puzzle_widget` type objects.
- **Mental items**: "Star Chart Notes" and "Melody Notes" are knowledge items — they're added to inventory when the player examines the relevant objects, but they represent understood information rather than physical objects. Use `"physical": false` in the item definition if the schema supports it, or treat them as flags.
- **Moral fork**: The three endings should be three separate `win_conditions`, each with distinct flag requirements. Taking the nightingale vs. leaving it vs. taking only the scroll should set different flags, and each ending triggers different `ending_text`.
- **View navigation**: Close-up views (workbench_closeup, clock_face_closeup, music_box_closeup, star_chart_closeup, tool_chest_closeup) should each have `back_label` and `parent_view` set to their parent view.
